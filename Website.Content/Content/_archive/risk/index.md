The argument for pausing does not depend on science fiction. It depends on
three facts that are not seriously disputed by anyone building these systems.

## Nobody knows how they work

A modern AI model is not written. It is **grown** — trained by adjusting
billions of numbers until the output looks right. The result works, and no one
can say why. Interpretability research, which tries to read what is actually
happening inside a model, is years behind the capabilities it is trying to
explain, and the gap is widening rather than closing.

This is an unusual position for an engineering discipline. We do not ship
bridges we cannot analyse. We are shipping minds we cannot inspect.

## Capability is rising faster than control

Every year models get better at things nobody trained them to do. They write
working code, pass professional exams, use tools, operate computers, and
increasingly act over long horizons without a human in the loop. Each of those
was, at some point, a capability researchers expected to be years away.

Meanwhile the techniques for keeping a system aligned with what we actually
want have not kept pace. We can make a model refuse an obvious request. We
cannot yet guarantee that a much more capable system pursuing a goal will
pursue the goal we meant.

## The people building it say so out loud

This is the part that tends to change minds. The warnings are not coming from
outside the industry:

- The CEOs of **OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind** all signed a statement
  putting extinction risk from AI alongside pandemics and nuclear war.
- **Geoffrey Hinton** left Google to speak freely about it, and later won a
  Nobel Prize.
- Internal estimates of catastrophic risk from senior lab staff are routinely
  in the single-digit to double-digit percentages. Those are not the odds you
  accept on a bridge.

A company that believed its product had a five per cent chance of ending
civilisation and shipped it anyway would, in any other sector, be the subject
of a criminal inquiry. In AI it is the subject of a funding round.

## Why a race makes it worse

None of the above requires anyone to be reckless or malicious. It only
requires them to be **competing**. If safety work slows you down and your
competitor does not slow down, the safe lab loses — so the pressure runs one
way, toward shipping sooner with less testing. Every individual decision looks
defensible. The aggregate is a race nobody chose and nobody can unilaterally
leave.

That is precisely the shape of problem that regulation exists to solve. Not
because companies are wicked, but because a race to the bottom cannot be fixed
from inside it.

## What this is not

- **Not a claim that AI is useless or should be banned.** Narrow systems that
  fold proteins or read scans are not the issue.
- **Not a claim that doom is certain.** It is a claim that the odds are
  unacceptable and nobody is managing them.
- **Not a claim that today's chatbots are dangerous.** The concern is the next
  few training runs, and the ones after that.
