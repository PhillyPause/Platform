## What the systems can do

The frontier has moved from *answering questions* to *doing work*. Current
systems write and debug substantial software, operate a computer through its
interface, run multi-step research tasks over hours, and increasingly act as
agents that call other tools and other models without a person checking each
step.

The significant change is not any single capability. It is **autonomy over
time**. A system that needs a human every thirty seconds is a tool. A system
that can be handed a goal and left alone for a day is something else, and the
labs are explicitly building the second thing.

## What the labs say they are building

They are not coy about it. The stated objective at the frontier is not better
chatbots — it is systems that can do most cognitive work a person can do, and
then systems that exceed that. Company roadmaps, investor material and
executive interviews all describe automating large fractions of economically
valuable work as the goal, on timelines measured in years.

Take that seriously for a moment, in either direction. If they are wrong, the
investment is a bubble. If they are right, we are a few years from the most
consequential technology in human history arriving with no safety case, no
regulator, and no off switch.

## Why "we will deal with it later" does not work

Three reasons the window is narrower than it feels:

1. **Capability jumps are discontinuous.** Models acquire abilities abruptly
   during training, not smoothly. You do not get a gentle warning.
2. **Deployment is irreversible.** Open weights cannot be recalled. A model
   released is a model available permanently, to everyone.
3. **Regulation takes years.** Drafting, passing and standing up enforcement
   for a technical regime is a multi-year project even with political will.
   Starting when the problem is obvious means finishing years after.

The lag between *obvious* and *handled* is the whole problem. Every month of
delay in starting is a month of delay in finishing.

## What has actually improved

It is not all bad news, and pretending otherwise is bad advocacy:

- Extinction risk from AI is now a mainstream position held by named,
  credentialed people, not a fringe one.
- Several jurisdictions have passed or drafted frontier-model rules, with
  compute thresholds and reporting duties.
- Public opinion is strongly and consistently in favour of slowing down,
  across the political spectrum.
- International summits now exist, which three years ago they did not.

What is missing is binding, enforced limits on the largest training runs.
That is [the ask](/ask), and it is the gap that
[contacting Congress](/act/congress) is aimed at.
