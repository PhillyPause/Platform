Some objections to a pause are bad. Several are good. Pretending otherwise
loses arguments with exactly the people worth convincing, so here are the real
ones.

## "China will not pause, so we would just be losing"

The strongest objection, and the most common.

Three things are true. First, this is an argument for an **international**
agreement, which is what we are asking for — not for unilateral disarmament.
Second, the hardware supply chain is narrow and heavily concentrated, which
makes multilateral enforcement far more tractable here than for, say, chemical
weapons. Third, Chinese official statements on AI risk have been consistently
more cautious than American ones, and Chinese regulation of deployed AI is
already stricter.

The honest version of the objection is not *China will refuse*. It is *nobody
has yet done the diplomatic work to find out*. That is a reason to start, not a
reason to shrug.

## "This will kill innovation"

A pause above a frontier compute threshold leaves almost all AI work
untouched. Medical imaging, protein structure prediction, translation, weather
modelling, accessibility tooling — none of these run at the frontier.

What is restricted is a specific, narrow, enormously expensive race between
about five companies to build a general system more capable than people. Saying
innovation requires that race is like saying medical progress requires
unregulated human trials.

## "It is unenforceable"

Frontier training needs tens of thousands of specialised chips, made by a
handful of companies, on lithography machines from essentially one firm. Those
chips are already export-controlled, already tracked, and already the subject
of international agreement.

Enforcement is not easy. It is a known category of hard problem that
governments have solved before, in nuclear materials and in precursor
chemicals.

## "The risk is overblown — these are just autocomplete"

Sometimes said about systems that pass the bar exam and write production code.
But take it seriously: maybe scaling stalls, maybe current approaches plateau
short of anything dangerous.

Then a pause costs very little. The argument for acting does not require
certainty that the risk is real; it requires that the risk is **plausible and
irreversible**. We buy insurance against house fires we do not expect.

## "Regulation would entrench the big labs"

A real risk, and one advocates should not wave away. Compliance costs favour
incumbents; a licensing regime written by the licensed tends to protect them.

It is an argument about **design**, not about whether to act. Thresholds based
on compute rather than revenue, independent rather than industry-staffed
evaluation, and liability that cannot be contracted away all cut against
capture. A badly designed pause could be captured. So could a badly designed
anything.

## "If it is really that dangerous, they would stop"

They have told us the odds and kept going. That is the part that should worry
you most, and it is the clearest evidence that the race dynamic is real: these
are not people who are unaware of the risk. They are people who cannot
unilaterally stop without losing.

Which is the whole case for an external, binding limit — see [the ask](/ask).

## The objection we do not have a good answer to

Timing. We cannot tell you how long there is, and anyone who claims to is
guessing. That cuts both ways: it undercuts urgency, and it undercuts
complacency. We have chosen to act as though the window is short, because the
cost of being wrong in that direction is a delay, and the cost of being wrong
in the other direction is unbounded.
