## Short, local, one ask

The people who read constituent mail read a great deal of it. What they look for first is whether you live in the district, then what you are asking for. Put both in the first two sentences. A good message fits on a phone screen.

## Your own words count for more

Offices can tell a form letter from a personal one, and they count them differently. Thousands of identical messages are logged as one campaign. A message that says, in your own words, why this matters to you is read, and is more likely to be answered.

That is why the [write page](/act) starts by asking why it matters to you. Everything else in the draft is scaffolding: change anything that does not sound like you.

## About the drafting help

When it is switched on, an AI model helps turn your notes into a first draft. We know how that sounds coming from us. It is there because a blank page stops more people than anything else does, not to speak for you. You read every word, you change what you want, and you send it yourself, from your own email or the office's own form. If you would rather write it from scratch, do: name, where you live, why, one ask.

## Email or the office's form

Most members of Congress do not publish an email address. They use a contact form on their website, which asks for your address so it can confirm you are a constituent. Paste your message into its message box. State legislators and City Council members more often publish an email address; the write page uses it when there is one.

## Ask for a reply

End by asking where they stand. Most offices reply eventually, often by letter or email weeks later. A reply is useful: it is a position on the record, and it is something to follow up on.

## Follow up

If two or three weeks pass with no answer, call and ask whether your message arrived. That call is often what produces a reply.
