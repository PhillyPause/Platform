A constituent sitting across the table is the form of contact staff consistently say carries the most weight. It is less intimidating than it sounds, and you will not have to do it alone: one of us has taken this to Congress more than seventy-five times, and we will come with you.

## Asking for one

Every office has a way to request a meeting, usually a scheduling form on its website, or the district office's phone number. Say who you are, that you are a constituent, what you would like to discuss, and how many people would come. A meeting with a staff member, often the legislative assistant who covers technology, is normal and valuable: they brief the member.

District offices are easier to get into than Washington, and the meeting can happen during a recess when the member is home.

## Who should go

Two to four constituents is ideal. More than that and nobody gets to talk. Agree beforehand who opens, who tells their own story, and who makes the ask.

## What to bring

One page to leave behind: who you are, what you are asking for, and two or three sourced facts. Your contact details at the bottom. We have a template, and will help you fit it to the office.

## In the room

- Thank them for the time, and say where each of you lives.
- Tell them, briefly, why this matters to you personally.
- Make the ask, plainly, once.
- Ask where the member stands, and listen.
- If they ask something you cannot answer, say you will find out, and then do.

Twenty minutes is typical. Keep to time.

## Afterwards

Send a thank-you email within a day or two, repeat the ask, and answer anything you promised to. Then tell us how it went, so the next group to visit that office knows where things stand.
